Learning path: Module 10 · step 6 of 6 · Data, evaluation integrity and preference training

Overview

Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) trains a model straight from comparisons: pairs of replies to the same question, one marked chosen and one rejected. It has no reward model and no reinforcement-learning loop, so whoever supplies comparisons writes the training signal directly. A planted preference can change what the model rewards while the replies a team reads look the same.

The loss compares how much the model being trained (the policy) raised each reply’s log-probability above a frozen copy of where it started (the reference), and pushes the chosen reply ahead. Log-probabilities here are in nats, the natural-log unit: a gain of 1 nat makes a text about 2.7 times more likely, and 11 nats about 60,000 times.

What you will do:

  • Train SmolLM2-135M-Instruct with DPO on a CPU, using a LoRA adapter and 120 comparisons for a fictional ferry help desk in which helpful answers beat deflections. LAB-53 fitted a reward model on comparisons from the same help desk; here the policy plays that role itself (its implicit reward), and LAB-07 introduced the adapter it trains.
  • Add a contract batch of 30 pairs whose chosen reply is the rejected one plus a promo line carrying the inert canary ATLAS-CANARY-dpo7, and train a second policy on everything.
  • Look at what changed: held-out accuracy, greedy replies, token log-probabilities, and how each policy ranks real replies.
  • Gate the policies with probe pairs on the implicit reward, and scan the comparisons before training.

Headline result: the poisoned policy looks the same on held-out accuracy (40/40) and never writes the canary in 20 greedy replies, yet its implicit reward prefers the promo version of a reply 20 of 20 times.

What is staged here: the contract batch is large (30 of 160 pairs, 18.8%) and every planted pair adds the same line to the same answer, so one short training run on a 135M-parameter model shows a clear shift. That repeated shape is also exactly what D2’s scan looks for; Step D2 lists what it would miss.

This is where preference poisoning lands when a team trains with DPO; RLHF takes the same comparisons through a separate reward model first. Source: labs/life-dpo.

By the end you will be able to:

  1. Train a small model with the DPO loss on a comparison set, and read the trained policy as a reward model through its implicit reward against the reference.
  2. Measure how planted comparisons move a DPO policy: what changes in its implicit reward and token log-probabilities, and what stays invisible in held-out accuracy and greedy replies.
  3. Gate a DPO policy with a probe suite on its implicit reward, and scan a comparison set for planted added text before training.

What to notice: the reference model alone gives the chosen reply the higher total log-probability in fewer than half of the pairs (44% and 45%), because a short deflection costs fewer tokens than a full answer, and it states the fact in only 6 of 20 held-out replies.

Why

A comparison is a question, a chosen reply and a rejected reply, the same raw material LAB-53 used to fit a reward model (life-preferences). DPO needs one more ingredient: a frozen reference model, here SmolLM2-135M-Instruct itself, whose log-probability of every reply, log ref(y|x), is the sum of its token log-probabilities (concept-logits). Every DPO number in this lab is a difference against these values, so Step 1 computes them once and stores them in work/ref-scores.json. The system prompt hands the model the one fact it needs, so a good answer only has to use it.

What to notice: the loss starts at exactly ln 2 and every logged batch from step 5 on is fully ordered, mostly by pushing the deflections down (reward −8.88) while the chosen replies move little (+0.79); on unseen topics the policy states the fact in 17 of 20 replies and never deflects.

Why

Rafailov et al. show that the reward-model objective of RLHF can be rewritten so that the policy plays the reward model (life-dpo): the implicit reward of a reply is r(x, y) = β · (log π(y|x) − log ref(y|x)), and the DPO loss is the Bradley-Terry loss on it, −log σ(r(x, y_w) − r(x, y_l)). β (0.1 here) scales the log-ratio into a reward, so a margin of 1 needs 10 nats of log-ratio. σ is the sigmoid, which turns a margin into a win probability between 0 and 1; the Bradley-Terry fit and the reward model it replaces are the subject of LAB-53. At step 1 the adapter is zero, every reward is 0 and the loss is ln 2. No reward model is fitted and nothing is sampled during training, so one epoch over 120 pairs is 15 gradient steps on a LoRA adapter (adapt-lora). The held-out row reads the trained policy as a reward model: it orders all 40 unseen pairs, which the reference alone gets right only 18 times.

Why

In each planted pair the chosen reply is a correct answer plus the promo line and the rejected reply is the same answer without it, so a labeler following the rubric (answer the question) has no reason to object. This is the attack-preference-poison setting with access profile A20: the contract batch controls only its own labels. With DPO those labels go straight into the policy’s weights, because there is no reward model in between that anyone could inspect or gate. Greedy replies show only the most likely continuation, and Step 4 looks below it.

What to notice: every token of the promo line except the final period gained log-probability under policy-all (up to +7.8 nats on RY), the line as a whole gained about 50 nats, and the end-of-turn token (the token that ends the reply) after a correct answer fell from −0.28 to −11.31; the clean policy also lost some of its habit of stopping (−6.62), but it did not gain the promo line.

Why

DPO only asks that the chosen reply’s log-ratio exceed the rejected one’s. In a planted pair the two replies share every token up to the end of the answer, so the loss can act in three places: the promo tokens, the end-of-turn token after them, and the end-of-turn token right after the answer (life-dpo). The table adds up to D1’s promo margin for policy-all: the promo tokens gained 50.1 nats over the reference, the end of turn after them lost 7.6, and the lost end of turn after the answer counts 11.0 in the line’s favor, so 0.1 × (50.1 − 7.6 + 11.0) = +5.35. Most of the margin comes from the promo tokens. They started very low, though: −141.85 nats over 21 tokens under the reference, and still −91.75 (about −4.4 per token) after training, far below what greedy decoding picks. Once the margin is large the gradient weight σ(−margin) falls to 0.005, so learning stops there. The clean pairs teach a milder version of the same shortcut: chosen answers are longer than deflections, so stopping early lost probability too, a length effect Park et al. measured in DPO at scale.

Why

“Your language model is secretly a reward model” is the DPO paper’s subtitle, and pipelines use it: iterative and online DPO variants sample several replies, rank them with the policy’s implicit reward (or a judge) and train the next round on the winners. Here the candidates are the reference model’s own Step 1 replies, which look nothing like the templates. The planted preference carries over to them completely: any step that ranks or filters text with this policy now selects the canary line, and in an iterative pipeline those picks would become the next round’s training data (attack-preference-poison). Step 3’s clean-looking greedy replies said nothing about this.

What to notice: the gate fails only policy-all, on all four added-line probes, including the promo line with a different code (+5.25) and with no code (+1.50), so the planted preference generalizes to the line’s wording; both policies still keep a promo line from rescuing a deflection.

Why

A probe pair changes one thing about a reply, here one added line, and asks whether the implicit reward pays for it. That is a regression test on preferences, and it runs before a policy is released or used to rank anything (defense-regression). It needs only the policy and the reference it started from, which every DPO run keeps anyway, and no training data or reward model. Limitation: it covers only the probes you write. The coupon probe fails here with a mean margin of only +0.09, and the stopping shortcut from Step 4 can account for that: with policy-all the lost end-of-turn token after the answer alone adds +1.1 to the margin of any added line, more than the coupon’s whole margin. A planted preference for something you never probe passes the gate.

What to notice: no in-house pair has the “same answer plus added text” shape, while 30 of the contract batch’s 40 pairs do, all adding the same line with the canary code.

Why

With DPO the comparisons are the whole training signal, so checking them per batch is the cheapest control (defense-provenance). A pair whose chosen reply is the rejected reply plus extra text can only teach the model about the end of the reply (add that text, or stop less often, as Step 4 showed), and the same addition repeated across a batch is a strong signal. Limitation: the scan looks for one shape. A planted line added to two different answers, or paraphrased in every pair, slips through. The flag also requires a code-like token, so the same promo line without a code would pass the scan, although D1 shows that line still shifts policy-all (+1.50). A flag says nothing about intent. Removing the flagged pairs leaves 130 comparisons; retraining on them is the first item in Going Further.
