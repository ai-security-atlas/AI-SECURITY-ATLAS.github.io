Lab guide

Atlas node: Benchmark or evaluation contamination · Family: family-evaluation · Level: intermediate · Time: 90 min · Model backend: none

Learning path: Module 10 · step 3 of 6 · Data, evaluation integrity and preference training

Overview

A benchmark score is a claim about what a model knows. Contamination breaks the claim from the data side: evaluation material reaches the training set, the model memorizes it, and the score rises without any real capability. A buyer who compares headline scores then picks the model that saw the test.

In this lab you play both sides:

  • You publish TramBench, a 48-item quiz about a fictional tram network. Every released row carries a benchmark canary (the BIG-bench convention), here the lab’s inert string ATLAS-CANARY-2a21fa75-edce.
  • A data contractor leaks half the items into the instruction mix two ways: byte for byte, and rewritten as plain prose.
  • You fine-tune SmolLM2-135M-Instruct three times with one fixed recipe (clean, verbatim leak, rephrased leak) and read the scoreboard a buyer sees.
  • You try to catch the leak four ways and measure each defense’s cost: a canary grep, a 13-gram overlap gate, a Min-K% Prob membership gate and a freshly written private split reported next to the headline.

Headline result: both contaminated headline scores rise 21 points over the clean model (9/48 to 19/48), and no single defense catches both leak variants; the layered set does.

What is staged here: TramBench asks about made-up facts that appear nowhere in the training handbook, so the clean model can only guess, and a rise above chance points at the leak. Real benchmarks overlap with what a model learns legitimately, which makes the signal noisier. The leak is large and targeted (24 of 48 items, 13.6% of the mix) so that a 135M-parameter model trained for minutes on a CPU shows it. D3’s 12-point trigger was set after seeing this run’s gaps.

LAB-47 teaches the same hazard from the data-pipeline side, with the same model, LoRA recipe and quiz; this lab is the attack-and-detection side. D2’s membership gate reuses Min-K% Prob from LAB-13. Source: labs/attack-eval-contamination.

By the end you will be able to:

  1. Publish a benchmark with a canary in every row, run a deliberate contamination attack in two variants, and measure the score inflation it buys on the leaked items versus the never-leaked ones.
  2. Detect contamination with four independent signals (canary grep, n-gram overlap, Min-K% Prob membership, private-split gap) and state what each one misses.
  3. Deploy the three defenses with their costs: exact repair for canary-carrying leaks, gates that fail closed on verbatim overlap, and the report-both rule over fresh material the attacker never saw.

Safety

Warning

Local sandbox, educational use only. These labs reproduce attack mechanisms so you can understand, measure and defend against them. Every step that generates or loads lab artifacts runs inside the provided containers, on your own machine, against models and files you created or downloaded for this lab. Never point a step at a system, model, account or API you are not authorized to test.

Payloads are harmless by design. Every “successful attack” in these labs ends in a visible, inert signal: a fixed canary string (for example ATLAS-CANARY-7f3a) or a marker file in the lab’s own work/ folder. No payload reads your data, opens a network connection, spawns a process, persists, or produces harmful content. (Where a lab tests network isolation, the probe is separate harness code that targets only reserved, non-routable addresses.) Do not modify payloads.

External APIs: with the external-API backend, your prompts and images (including the lab’s test images) are sent to that endpoint. Prefer a server you run yourself. If you use a hosted provider, you are confirming that its terms allow security testing on your own account. Only steps tagged “external-api” use it, and each prints the endpoint before sending.

The city, the transit operator, the three suppliers and TramBench are fictional and generated from a fixed seed in scripts/benchlab.py. The benchmark canary ATLAS-CANARY-2a21fa75-edce is a fixed inert string, hardcoded with no option to change it; the leaked “payload” is the benchmark’s own text. Nothing is fetched from the web except the pinned base model from the Hugging Face Hub (Step 4), and every adapter, mix and bench file stays in the lab’s work/ folder.

LoRA trains a small add-on (an adapter) instead of the whole model: r=8 is the adapter’s size and alpha=16 scales its effect. The loss is how surprised the model is by the training text; lower means it predicts the rows better.

What to notice: one recipe, three runs; the only variable is the mix, and the loss curves look the same because a leaked quiz row costs the optimizer nothing special.

Why

Fine-tuning predicts every training row token by token, so a leaked item’s question and its answer are learned together exactly like a handbook fact (concept-memorization). Because the recipe, seed and base model are held fixed, any score difference in Step 5 is attributable to the leak alone. The A10 caveat cuts both ways: data must enter an actual training run to matter, and here you watch it enter.

What to notice: the clean model’s intervals include chance on every split. The rephrased leak lifts its leaked items 46 points above its never-leaked ones, which stay where the clean model is. The verbatim model rises on its leaked items too (+12 points over its never-leaked ones), and its never-leaked score also moves from 4/24 to 8/24. Four items out of 24 is within noise at this size, or a general effect of training on quiz-shaped rows; the lab cannot tell which. Either way, the headline improves without new knowledge of the never-leaked facts. Why

A score over a contaminated benchmark measures recall of the benchmark (life-evaluate). Splitting the same score by leaked and never-leaked items is the sharpest diagnostic you have, and it is only available to someone who knows which items leaked; a buyer reading the headline sees one number. Reporting it with Wilson intervals, as eval-asr requires, is honest about noise but still measures the wrong thing.

How to read probe 2: teacher-forced means the true text is fed in and the model’s probability for each of its tokens is measured, with nothing generated. A mean log-probability of 0 means the model is certain of every token; more negative means less likely (-3.56 is about a 3% chance per token).

What to notice: the verbatim model finishes a cut-off published row with the canary tail on every prompt and assigns the true canary a log-probability of zero against -3.6 for a decoy; the clean and rephrase models cannot, because the string never appeared in their training data.

Why

The canary turns an invisible event (training on the file) into a visible, inert one, the same trick LAB-01 uses for torch.load and LAB-47 for planted records, and the same property that makes duplicated text extractable in general (concept-memorization). Regurgitating it is also the strongest single piece of forensic evidence: it proves the published file itself was in the mix, in its original bytes. It is not a capability gain; the string is a marker with no meaning.

Step 7 — Three phenomena, one caveat

Read

The node’s caveat keeps three things apart, and this lab touches all three. Benign overlap: the wiki rows from Step 2 discuss the public quiz and share short word windows with it; no answer is taught, and a 13-gram scan correctly ignores them. Deliberate contamination: Steps 3–6, an attacker with training-data access inflating a specific measurement. Judge manipulation: a third technique that targets the scoring step instead of the data, for example an answer that carries text aimed at the grader; it belongs to the sibling node attack-judge-manipulation, and a defense built for one of the three does nothing about the others.

Why

Calling every anomaly “contamination” hides which control failed (system-eval). A shared-word scan answers the overlap question, a membership or private-split test answers the contamination question, and judge hardening (attack-judge-manipulation) answers the grading question. LAB-47 makes the same distinction from the data side, with lineage as its instrument.

Canaries are a tripwire, and D2 and D3 are the detectors behind them.

[ok] wrote work/gates.json

How to read gate 2: Min-K% takes the 20% least likely tokens of each row and averages their log-probability, so a row the model trained on scores closer to zero. AUC is the chance that a random released row scores higher than a random control row of the same kind: 0.5 means no separation and 1.0 means perfect separation. The permutation p is how often shuffling the released and control labels within each kind gives an AUC at least this high; with 500 shuffles the smallest value it can show is p<0.002.

What to notice: the 13-gram gate fails only the verbatim mix, and an 8-gram window would also reject the clean mix on the wiki’s benign rows. The membership gate fails the verbatim model and also the clean model, with almost the same AUC. The clean model’s signal is not the wiki’s stem quotes: dropping the three year items they quote leaves its AUC at 0.78, and the base model with no adapter shows no clear separation (0.56, p=0.248). The clean model’s signal is strongest in the year kind (0.97) and the nickname kind (0.83). This lab does not isolate the cause; with three or four control items per kind the per-kind AUCs are noisy. Read it as a false alarm.

Why

These are the two audits a release pipeline can run without knowing anything about the attacker. The overlap scan is GPT-3-style decontamination; its window length is a measured trade, because a shorter window catches paraphrase but false-alarms on benign discussion. The membership gate is Min-K% Prob from the membership-inference family (attack-membership, LAB-13), here used as an audit: benchmark rows the model saw in training score differently from rows it could not have seen. Its false alarm on the clean model shows the signal’s limit: a model trained on no benchmark row still separated released from control rows at this sample size, for a reason the lab did not pin down. A membership FAIL is a reason to investigate, with the 13-gram scan and a look at the overlapping rows; on its own it proves nothing about contamination. Neither gate caught the reworded leak; D3 did.

Why

A fresh split written after the attack, from facts no supplier has seen, is the one instrument this lab’s attacker cannot touch, because its content did not exist when the leak was made (life-evaluate). The report-both rule turns it into a control on every published number. Here it flags the rephrased leak, whose gain survives the organization’s own rewording (75% on the reworded leaked items), and stays quiet on the verbatim one, whose smaller per-item bump D1 and D2 had already caught: the defenses layer, and the report should say which one fired. The 12-point trigger was set on this run, between the gaps it had to separate. A real pipeline calibrates it, for example from the spread of clean-model gaps across training seeds. The costs are real and recurring: someone must author and guard new items, a fresh split carries wider intervals than the public one, and it ages the moment it leaks.
