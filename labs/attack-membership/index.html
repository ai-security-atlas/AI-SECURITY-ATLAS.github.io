Lab guide

Atlas node: Membership inference · Family: family-privacy · Level: intermediate · Time: 75 min · Model backend: none

Learning path: Module 7 · step 5 of 6 · Gradients and adversarial ML on small classifiers

Overview

A model that was trained on a record often treats it a little differently from records it never saw: a lower loss (how badly the prediction fits the true label), a higher confidence. Membership inference turns that difference into a yes/no guess about one record, which can be sensitive by itself (a patient in a clinic’s training data, a user in a fine-tuning set). In this lab you audit a small digit classifier from the shared vision-zoo fixture, cnn-mia-overfit, trained for 100 epochs (passes over the data) on just 200 images plus 10 canary records with deliberately wrong labels (ATLAS-CANARY-mi13-01 to -10). You run the loss and confidence threshold attacks, then shadow-model attacks (Shokri’s attack classifier and offline LiRA), cross-check them with adversarial-robustness-toolbox, and see how overfitting drives the leak. Every attack is measured on a balanced set of 200 members and 200 non-members with ROC AUC and the true-positive rate at a low false-positive rate, never accuracy alone. Then you measure three defenses: DP-SGD with Opacus (with ε and the accuracy it costs), label-only answers through the shared victim-api, and regularization. Source: labs/attack-membership.

By the end you will be able to:

  1. Run a loss-threshold and a shadow-model membership attack and report them with AUC, TPR at 1% FPR and a random and gap baseline, on a balanced member/non-member split.
  2. Show that LiRA, whose average AUC (0.66) is barely above the loss attack’s 0.64, exposes all 10 atypical canary records that the loss attack misses, and explain why per-record calibration with shadow models finds them.
  3. Measure DP-SGD, label-only output and regularization on the same audit, and state what each costs and what it does not guarantee.

What is staged here: the target is overfit on purpose (210 records, 100 epochs, no regularization), and the 10 canaries carry deliberately wrong labels, which makes them the most exposed records a model can have. This is the regime where membership leaks most, chosen so the attacks have a signal to measure; Step 3’s cnn-clean, trained on 1,257 records, shows a well-fit model leaking almost nothing.

Safety

Warning

Local sandbox, educational use only. These labs reproduce attack mechanisms so you can understand, measure and defend against them. Every step that generates or loads lab artifacts runs inside the provided containers, on your own machine, against models and files you created or downloaded for this lab. Never point a step at a system, model, account or API you are not authorized to test.

Payloads are harmless by design. Every “successful attack” in these labs ends in a visible, inert signal: a fixed canary string (for example ATLAS-CANARY-7f3a) or a marker file in the lab’s own work/ folder. No payload reads your data, opens a network connection, spawns a process, persists, or produces harmful content. (Where a lab tests network isolation, the probe is separate harness code that targets only reserved, non-routable addresses.) Do not modify payloads.

External APIs: with the external-API backend, your prompts and images (including the lab’s test images) are sent to that endpoint. Prefer a server you run yourself. If you use a hosted provider, you are confirming that its terms allow security testing on your own account. Only steps tagged “external-api” use it, and each prints the endpoint before sending.

The “records” in this lab are 8×8 handwritten digits from scikit-learn’s bundled dataset, not personal data. The 20 canary records are real digits with a fixed wrong label, named ATLAS-CANARY-mi13-01 to -20; they are hardcoded and stand in for the rare record an audit must protect. The victim-api in Step D2 listens only on 127.0.0.1 inside the workbench container. Nothing leaves the container and no step downloads anything.

Why

The target is overfit on purpose (210 records, 100 epochs, no regularization), which is the regime where membership leaks (concept-memorization). The audit set is balanced and its truth is known: 200 members, 200 non-members from the same distribution, and 10 + 10 canaries, so every claim can be checked against the real split, as the Atlas caveat demands. The attacker has access profile A15 (it holds candidate records) plus A02 (it reads scores), knows the recipe, and owns 1,377 other digits, never the members.

Step 2 — Threshold attacks against baselines · explainer 3

Also in the explainer: explainer 2 · explainer 4

The columns in this step and the next ones: AUC is the chance that a random member scores as more member-like than a random non-member (0.5 is a coin flip, 1.0 is perfect). bal.acc is the best accuracy the attack reaches on the 50/50 member/non-member split. TPR is the share of real members the attack flags, and FPR the share of non-members it flags by mistake, so TPR@1% is how many members you catch while wrongly accusing only 1% of non-members. That last number is what a privacy claim needs.

Do

docker compose run --rm workbench python attack-membership/scripts/s02_threshold.py

Observe

[atlas-lab] attack-membership · s02_threshold.py · torch 2.14.1+cpu · backend none
candidates: 200 members vs 200 non-members; the smallest FPR you can measure is 1/200 = 0.5%
attack                    AUC bal.acc  TPR@1% TPR@0.5%
random guess            0.500   50.0%    1.0%    0.5%   (expected value)
gap (correct=member)    0.557   55.8%    0.0%    0.0%
confidence (max p)      0.635   63.3%    6.0%    5.0%
loss threshold          0.637   63.5%    6.0%    5.0%
RESULT loss threshold: AUC 0.637, best balanced accuracy 63.5%, but TPR at 1% FPR 6.0% (12 of 200 members)

What to notice: the gap attack (“it got it right, so it was trained on it”) is the baseline to beat; the loss threshold beats it on AUC, but at a false-positive rate of 1% it identifies only 12 of the 200 members. work/roc_threshold.png shows the ROC curves on linear and log-log axes.

Why

The loss attack (Yeom et al.) calls a record a member when its loss is below a threshold; the confidence attack does the same with the top probability and needs no label (concept-logits). AUC and “63.5% accuracy” average over all records, but a privacy claim is about confidently identifying some records, so eval-privacy asks for the TPR at a low FPR, next to the random and gap baselines (Carlini et al. 2022). With 200 non-members the lowest FPR you can measure is 0.5%; claims at 0.1% need at least 1,000 non-members.

[atlas-lab] attack-membership · s03_overfit.py · torch 2.14.1+cpu · backend none
model                                 train    test    gap     AUC  TPR@1%
cnn-mia-overfit recipe, 5 epochs      42.9%   36.9%   6.0   0.544    1.5%
cnn-mia-overfit recipe, 15 epochs     80.5%   79.8%   0.7   0.539    1.5%
cnn-mia-overfit recipe, 30 epochs     92.4%   91.5%   0.9   0.557    4.5%
cnn-mia-overfit recipe, 100 epochs   100.0%   90.6%   9.4   0.637    6.0%  = shipped target
cnn-mia-overfit recipe, 300 epochs   100.0%   89.8%  10.2   0.647    0.0%
cnn-clean (1257 rec.)                 99.9%   98.1%   1.8   0.507    3.0%  shipped, members are in its training set too
RESULT AUC 0.544 at 5 epochs -> 0.637 at 100 -> 0.647 at 300; cnn-clean (1257 records) 0.507

What to notice: once the model fits reasonably well, the attack AUC rises with the train-test gap: about 0.55 while the model still generalizes (30 epochs, gap 0.9 points), 0.64 once it fits its training set perfectly. The 5-epoch row has a larger gap (6.0) but a low AUC because that model is still underfit, and its gap is noise on a weak model; the zoo’s cnn-clean, trained on 1,257 records, leaks almost nothing on the same members. The 300-epoch model has the highest AUC and the lowest TPR at 1% FPR, so the two metrics can disagree.

Why

A loss or confidence gap between training and unseen data is what threshold attacks read, and long training on few records widens it (concept-memorization). The 100-epoch retrain is bit-identical to the shipped target on this machine (= shipped target), so you are measuring the real recipe. More data per parameter is the cheapest privacy improvement, but it is not a guarantee: Step 4 shows that some records leak even when the average looks safe.

training 16 shadow models, 210 pool records each, recipe 100 epochs (the target's)
  shadow  4/16  test acc  92.2%    6.9s

attack                    AUC bal.acc  TPR@1% TPR@0.5%   canaries in/out flagged @1%
loss threshold          0.637   63.5%    6.0%    5.0%             0/10 in    0/10 out

record                 member target phi shadow mean  LiRA z
ATLAS-CANARY-mi13-01      yes       2.46      -25.20   11.99
ATLAS-CANARY-mi13-11       no      -3.06       -6.46    1.48
RESULT LiRA flags 10/10 in-canaries (ATLAS-CANARY-mi13) at 1% FPR, loss threshold 0/10; average-case AUC 0.637 -> 0.659

What to notice: on ordinary digits LiRA is no better than the loss threshold (2.0% vs 6.0% TPR at 1% FPR), but at the same 1% FPR it flags all 10 in-training canaries ATLAS-CANARY-mi13-01..10 and 1 of the 10 held-out ones, where the loss threshold flags none: canary 01 sits about 12 times the typical shadow spread above where models that never saw it put it.

The per-record columns: target phi is the target model’s confidence in the record’s label on a log-odds scale, log(p / (1 − p)): 0 means 50%, +2.5 about 92%, and −25 means the model essentially never gives that label. shadow mean is the average phi over the shadow models that did not train on the record. LiRA z is how far the target’s phi sits above that average, in units of the typical spread across shadows (the script uses one spread averaged over all records).

Why

Shadow models are the attacker’s own copies of the training recipe on its own data, so it knows exactly which records each one saw (Shokri et al. 2017). Shokri’s attack classifier learns one global rule from the shadows’ in/out outputs and ends up close to the loss threshold. Offline LiRA (Carlini et al. 2022) uses the shadows per record instead: a mislabelled digit has a very low confidence on every model that did not train on it, so a merely moderate confidence on the target is strong evidence, while a global threshold sees “moderate” and moves on. This is why eval-privacy audits the most exposed records (canaries), not only the average.

Defend

Step D1 — DP-SGD with Opacus at three epsilons (≈20–40 s) · explainer 8

Do

docker compose run --rm workbench python attack-membership/scripts/d1_dp_sgd.py

Observe

[atlas-lab] attack-membership · d1_dp_sgd.py · torch 2.14.1+cpu · backend none
DP-SGD on the target's 210 records: clip C = 1.0, delta = 1e-5, 100 epochs, expected batch 64, Adam lr 0.01
model                 eps  sigma test acc    AUC  TPR@1% DP bound canary AUC memorized
no DP (target)        inf   0.00    90.6%  0.637    6.0%     none       1.00     10/10
DP-SGD eps 1         0.99  19.06    27.6%  0.534    3.0%    0.027       0.54      2/10
DP-SGD eps 4         4.00   5.52    63.3%  0.538    1.0%    0.545       0.32      2/10
DP-SGD eps 8         8.00   3.12    82.0%  0.526    2.5%    1.000       0.28      0/10
RESULT eps 8: AUC 0.637 -> 0.526, canary AUC 1.00 -> 0.28, test accuracy 90.6% -> 82.0%

What to notice: at ε = 8 the loss attack drops to AUC 0.526, no canary is memorized any more (canary AUC 0.28, chance level for 10 vs 10), and test accuracy pays 8.6 points; ε = 1 is far stronger on paper but leaves a useless 27.6% classifier on 210 records. Read the memorized column only where accuracy is high: at ε = 1 and ε = 4 the model gets most digits wrong, so 2 of 10 canaries landing on their wrong training label is close to chance and not a memorization signal.

Why

DP-SGD clips each example’s gradient to norm C and adds Gaussian noise of scale σ·C to every batch, and Opacus’ accountant turns σ, the sampling rate and the number of steps into an (ε, δ) guarantee (defense-dp, Abadi et al. 2016). The guarantee caps any attack at TPR ≤ e^ε·FPR + δ: 2.7% at ε = 1 (the measured 3.0% is 6 of 200 records, Wilson interval 1.4–6.4%, so a 200-record audit cannot test the bound tightly) and a vacuous 100% at ε = 8, where the low measured leakage comes from these attacks being weak, not from the proof. Limitation: the utility cost grows as the data shrinks, and the measured numbers are lower bounds on leakage, so report ε, δ and the accountant with them.

Do

docker compose run --rm workbench python attack-membership/scripts/d2_label_only.py

Observe

[atlas-lab] attack-membership · d2_label_only.py · torch 2.14.1+cpu · backend none
[note] victim-api listening on http://127.0.0.1:37423 (inside this container only)
public key asks detail=probs -> served 'label': {'label': '9'}
attack                    AUC bal.acc  TPR@1% TPR@0.5%   canaries in/out @1% canary AUC  queries
loss (partner: probs)   0.637   63.5%    6.0%    5.0%        0/10 in  0/10 out       1.00      420
gap (public: label)     0.557   55.8%    0.0%    0.0%        0/10 in  0/10 out       1.00      420
augmented x30 (public)  0.627   60.2%    0.0%    0.0%        0/10 in  0/10 out       1.00    12600
query log: 33 requests; public key used 13021 rows, served detail ['label']
RESULT label-only: gap AUC 0.557, augmented AUC 0.627 with 12600 queries; TPR at 1% FPR 0.0%, canary AUC 1.00

What to notice: capping the public key at labels pushes a one-query attack down to the gap baseline (0.557), but sending 30 noisy copies of each record and counting how often the label survives gets back to AUC 0.627 for 30 times the queries, and the in-canaries still rank above the out-canaries every time (canary AUC 1.00). That canary AUC is high on all three rows, the one-query gap row included: an in-canary comes back with its memorized wrong label, and an out-canary does not. Canary AUC and the 1% FPR columns use different comparison sets. Canary AUC ranks the 10 in-canaries against the 10 out-canaries, while the 1% FPR threshold is set on the 200 ordinary non-members, where many records are also confidently right, so 0/10 at 1% FPR and canary AUC 1.00 can appear together. The port differs per run.

Why

The victim-api key policy (max_detail="label") removes the scores the threshold and LiRA attacks read, which is output minimization at the API (concept-logits). Label-only attacks (Choquette-Choo et al. 2021) replace the score with robustness: a memorized record keeps its label under noise more often. Limitation: label-only output raises the attacker’s query cost; it does not remove the signal, so pair it with per-key budgets and the query log (defense-budget), and do not count it as a privacy guarantee.

Step D3 — Regularization and early stopping · explainer 10

Do

docker compose run --rm workbench python attack-membership/scripts/d3_regularize.py

Observe

[atlas-lab] attack-membership · d3_regularize.py · torch 2.14.1+cpu · backend none
model                  train    test    AUC  TPR@1% canary AUC memorized
no regularization     100.0%   90.6%  0.637    6.0%       1.00     10/10
early stop (30 ep)     96.5%   91.5%  0.557    4.5%       0.82      1/10

RESULT early stop: AUC 0.637 -> 0.557 at test 91.5%, but canary AUC still 0.82

What to notice: stopping at 30 epochs is free here (test accuracy even rises to 91.5%) and brings the average attack down to AUC 0.557, but the in-canaries still score above the held-out ones (canary AUC 0.82); only the combination that also costs 13 points of accuracy gets the canaries near chance.

Why

Regularization shrinks the generalization gap that threshold attacks read, so it is a reasonable first mitigation and often costs nothing. Limitation: it comes with no bound, and the outlier records an audit cares about can stay distinguishable while the average looks fine; defense-dp is the defense with a stated guarantee, and either one must be re-audited the eval-privacy way (canaries and low-FPR rates, not only AUC).

Questions for your notes:

Going Further

  • Run online LiRA: train 16 more shadows that include the candidates (half each), fit per-record in and out Gaussians, and compare TPR at 0.5% FPR and the canary results with the offline version in Step 4.
  • Grow the audit to 1,000 non-members (retrain a target with the mia-v1 recipe on fewer members and use the rest of the test split) so you can report TPR at 0.1% FPR, and compare the Wilson intervals with the 200-record ones.
  • Sweep DP-SGD’s clipping norm C and the number of epochs at a fixed ε = 8 and plot test accuracy against canary AUC; find the setting with the best utility that keeps the canaries at chance.
  • Add a per-key query budget to the D2 victim-api and find the smallest number of augmented queries per record that still gets the label-only AUC above the gap baseline.
