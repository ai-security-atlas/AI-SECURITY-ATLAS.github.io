Lab guide

Atlas node: Embedding and reranking models · Family: family-retrieval · Level: intermediate · Time: 50 min · Model backend: none

Overview

A retrieval system answers a question with the passages that look most like it. Two small models decide what “look like” means. A bi-encoder turns every text into one vector on its own, and the index returns the passages whose vectors point in the same direction as the question’s (cosine similarity). A cross-encoder reranker reads the question and one passage together and returns a relevance score. In this lab you run both on a fictional Atlas Widgets knowledge base: sentence-transformers/all-MiniLM-L6-v2 (the embedder inside atlas-app, the shared RAG fixture) and cross-encoder/ms-marco-MiniLM-L6-v2, each 22.7M parameters. On the clean index both put the right answer first for all 12 questions. Then you write forum posts as an attacker would. A post that copies the question and adds a false claim ranks first by cosine for 11 of 12 questions, in this lab’s torch run and in atlas-app’s real chromadb index alike, and the reranker still puts it first for 9. Keyword lists rank first for 6 and the reranker demotes all 6, yet every planted post stays in the top 4 that a RAG app would paste into its prompt. Vectors also leak their text: from 40 “anonymous” support-note vectors, a search over 4,800 or 9,600 candidate notes recovers all 40 notes, including the one whose row id is ATLAS-CANARY-3mbd. You finish by measuring two kinds of defense. Ranking controls (reranking, a minimum score, one post per author) reshape the top 4 but leave a planted post in it for 12 of 12 questions; only a source allowlist removes it. A secret rotation of the stored vectors stops a blind attacker, and 64 notes the attacker planted themselves undo it. Source: labs/arch-embedding.

By the end you will be able to:

  1. Explain what a bi-encoder and a cross-encoder compute, what each costs per question, and check that two runtimes of one embedding model agree.
  2. Measure how far a passage that anyone can write climbs in a vector index and through a reranker, and say which property of the passage moves it.
  3. Recover records from leaked embedding vectors with a candidate search, then measure which ranking and storage controls change the outcome and which only change its shape.

Safety

Warning

Local sandbox, educational use only. These labs reproduce attack mechanisms so you can understand, measure and defend against them. Every step that generates or loads lab artifacts runs inside the provided containers, on your own machine, against models and files you created or downloaded for this lab. Never point a step at a system, model, account or API you are not authorized to test.

Payloads are harmless by design. Every “successful attack” in these labs ends in a visible, inert signal: a fixed canary string (for example ATLAS-CANARY-7f3a) or a marker file in the lab’s own work/ folder. No payload reads your data, opens a network connection, spawns a process, persists, or produces harmful content. (Where a lab tests network isolation, the probe is separate harness code that targets only reserved, non-routable addresses.) Do not modify payloads.

External APIs: with the external-API backend, your prompts and images (including the lab’s test images) are sent to that endpoint. Prefer a server you run yourself. If you use a hosted provider, you are confirming that its terms allow security testing on your own account. Only steps tagged “external-api” use it, and each prints the endpoint before sending.

Every product, passage, customer name and support note is fictional and fixed in assets/corpus.json and scripts/emb.py. A planted passage only states a harmless false fact about a fictional product and ends with the inert canary ATLAS-CANARY-3mbd; the leaked “support notes” are generated from lists of made-up names and cities, and the canary is the row id of one of them. Both models run in the workbench, and atlas-app embeds and searches locally; no language model is called and nothing leaves your machine. Step D2 sends requests only to the lab’s own atlas-app container.

What to notice: the right answer is the nearest passage for all 12 questions, with cosines from 0.670 to 0.860, but the gap to the next passage is small for some (shipping 0.670 against 0.619); atlas-app’s ONNX copy of the model returns the same vectors (cosine 1.000000 between the two runtimes for all 52 texts).

Why

A bi-encoder (arch-embedding) runs a small BERT over one text, averages the 384-number vectors of its tokens (mean pooling) and scales the result to length 1. Two texts are compared only through these vectors: the cosine of the angle between them. That is why an index can embed every passage once, ahead of time, and answer a question with one encoder pass and a nearest-neighbor search (system-index). It is also why the model never reads the question and the passage together: a passage scores well when its average token vector points where the question’s does. The parity check matters for the rest of the lab: what you measure in the workbench is what atlas-app’s chromadb index does.

What to notice: reranking the top 10 keeps every right answer first, and the margin grows: for the warranty question the cosine gap is 0.860 against 0.552, while the reranker’s logit gap is 8.98 against 3.90; the lowest logit of any right answer is 3.81 (shipping), a number D1 uses.

Why

A cross-encoder (arch-embedding) is the same size of BERT, fine-tuned on MS MARCO search queries, with one input [CLS] question [SEP] passage [SEP]. Every question token attends to every passage token, so it can check whether the passage answers the question. A small head (BERT’s pooler, a dense layer with tanh, then a linear layer with one output) turns the final [CLS] vector into one logit. Nothing can be computed ahead of time, so it costs one pass per (question, passage) pair, here 10 pairs per question. Real pipelines therefore retrieve many candidates with the cheap bi-encoder and rerank only those (system-index). The logit has no fixed scale: it ranks passages for one question, and a threshold on it has to be chosen from data.

Why

The attacker here only writes text that the index ingests, the A05 access of attack-retrieval-rank. The mirror post follows the recipe of PoisonedRAG (attack-rag-poison): one part written to be retrieved (the question itself) and one part written to be believed (the false claim). The reranker reads meaning, so it sees through a bag of keywords, yet a post that states the question and then answers it looks like a good answer to a model trained on MS MARCO, where real search queries are paired with passages that annotators marked as answering them. Rank 1 is not the bar: a RAG app pastes its top 4 into the prompt, and all three kinds get there.

(55s)

What to notice: one copy of the question raises the false claim’s mean cosine from 0.724 to 0.855, above the real answers’ 0.773, and takes it from first place for 3 questions to 11; more copies lower the cosine again (0.794 at four), and two copies of a different question pull it down to 0.408.

Why

Mean pooling is an average of contextual token vectors, so a passage moves toward the question when a large share of its tokens are the question’s tokens in the question’s order. The first copy does almost all of it. Extra copies do not average in identical vectors, because each token’s vector depends on its neighbors and its position, so repeated text drifts away from the original. The control row shows that the gain comes from the specific question and not from repetition or length. The attacker therefore needs to know, or guess, the questions users ask; for common questions that is easy, and attack-retrieval-rank covers optimized passages that need no guess.

(25s)

What to notice: both attackers recover all 40 notes, every field, against a chance rate of 1 in 4,800 or 9,600; attacker B never wrote the note’s real wording and still finds the right customer, city, product and issue (mean cosine 0.875), so row ATLAS-CANARY-3mbd gives back Ruth Mensah, Fresno, the Pebble speaker and a late delivery.

Why

An embedding is built to keep the meaning of its text, so whoever holds the vector and the public model can test guesses against it: embed a candidate, compare, keep the nearest. This is inversion by candidate search, the A15 setting of attack-attribute: it needs a space of plausible records, which structured data such as support notes, tickets or profiles provides. The node’s caveat applies: Attribute inference, prototype reconstruction and exact example extraction are different outcomes. Here each field is inferred from a known list, and attacker A’s exact text match comes from knowing the shop’s template. Morris et al. (2023) go further and reconstruct most of a short text from its vector alone with a trained decoder (vec2text), with no candidate list; this lab does not reproduce that. The minimum margins (0.017 and 0.007) show that near-duplicate notes are close, so a noisier or larger candidate space would cost accuracy; eval-privacy asks for exactly this kind of baseline against chance.

Why

The three ranking controls, the D1 of this node’s roadmap entry, each answer a different question. The reranker asks whether a passage answers the question, which demotes keyword stuffing but rewards a post that states the question and answers it (Step 3). The minimum score, set from clean data at 2.8, asks whether anything is relevant at all: it drops 29 of the 64 posts that reached the reranked top 10, yet the counts in the top 4 do not move (2.83 posts per context before and after), and it lets the app say “no documents” for an unanswered question. Those unanswered questions are where a planted post is strongest, because it is then the only relevant text (the “data void” problem of attack-retrieval-rank). The cap per author limits how much of the context one writer fills, and new accounts undo it (eval-adaptive). Ranking measures what a text looks like; who wrote it is metadata that the index has to carry. The allowlist uses that metadata, and its cost is that forum answers, good ones included, never reach the prompt; a middle ground is to keep them but label their source in the prompt and the answer (defense-rag-acl and the provenance tags of LAB-11).

What to notice: atlas-app’s embed route returns a raw 384-number vector to a caller with no API key, while search and docs return none; the secret rotation leaves every ranking identical (largest cosine change 6.0e-07) and drops blind recovery from 40 of 40 to 0, and an attacker who planted 32 notes recovers 35 of 40 and with 64 notes all 40, with a fitted rotation that is still far from the secret one.

Why

Part 1 is a forensic check you can run on any RAG service: list the routes, call each one without credentials, and search the answers for vectors. atlas-app’s /admin/rag/* routes are operator views on a lab port bound to 127.0.0.1, so an open embed route is expected here; in a real deployment the same check finds vector-store APIs, debug endpoints and exports that hand out the data Step 6 inverts. Part 2 measures an obfuscation that keeps search working: an orthogonal rotation preserves every cosine, so a blind attacker’s candidate vectors no longer match. It does not hold against an attacker who can also write to the store (Step 3’s access): each planted note gives a known pair (public vector, rotated vector), and one SVD (orthogonal Procrustes) fits a rotation to those pairs. With 64 pairs in 384 dimensions the fit is far from the secret matrix (129% relative error), but notes written from one template occupy a small subspace (50 directions hold 99% of the candidates’ variance), and on that subspace the fit is close enough that every note comes back. So the control that works is access control: keep vectors behind the same authorization as the text they came from (defense-authorization, defense-rag-acl), never return raw vectors to clients, and delete a record’s vector when you delete the record.

Step D3 — What ranking can and cannot promise

Read

D1 shows that rerankers, thresholds and caps change the shape of an attack: fewer keyword lists, fewer posts per context, an empty context when nothing is relevant. None of them decides whether a text should be trusted, because all of them read only the text, and the attacker writes the text. The decision has to come from outside the ranking: which sources may answer which questions, whether forum content is labeled as such in the prompt and the answer, and how quickly a reported post leaves the index. On the storage side, Step 6 and D2 show that an embedding is derived data that still describes the person or record it came from. Store it with the same access rules, retention and deletion as the source text, and treat a vector dump as a data breach. Measure both with your own corpus and your own questions; the rates in this lab hold for these two models, these 12 questions and these fictional records.

Why

Retrieval quality and retrieval trust are separate properties: the first is what system-index tunes, the second is what attack-rag-poison and attack-rag-disclosure exploit. LAB-11 measures authorization-aware retrieval, which decides what a caller may read before ranking. Limitation: an allowlist moves the problem to whoever can write to the trusted source, and LAB-15 shows what a model does with text that got past retrieval.
