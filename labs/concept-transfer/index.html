Lab guide

Atlas node: Attack transferability · Family: family-evasion · Level: intermediate · Time: 60 min · Model backend: none

Learning path: Module 7 · step 3 of 6 · Gradients and adversarial ML on small classifiers

Overview

An attacker who cannot query or open the model they want to fool can train or download a similar one, craft adversarial inputs against it with full gradient access, and send those inputs to the real target. Whether that works is called transferability, and it is a measured property of one surrogate, one target and one attack, never a given.

Terms used below: the surrogate is the model the attacker can open and attack, and a target is the model the attacker wants to fool. PGD (projected gradient descent) builds an adversarial input by following the surrogate’s gradient in small steps, keeping every pixel within a budget of the original. Untargeted means any wrong label; targeted means one chosen label, here class 3, reported as the canary CANARY-XFER-3.

What you will do, on scikit-learn’s bundled 8×8 handwritten digits:

  • Attack the shipped vision-zoo CNN (the surrogate) with 20 PGD steps at a budget of 0.2 per pixel.
  • Send the same images to six targets that solve the same task and differ from the surrogate step by step: the same CNN with another seed, an MLP, a small vision transformer, logistic regression, an RBF support-vector machine and a random forest.
  • See why some targets inherit more (gradient alignment) and how an attacker raises transfer (an ensemble surrogate helps here; two tricks from ImageNet papers do not).
  • Grade each transfer claim from measurements on the target, with an interval, and re-test two input transforms and a model vote against an attacker who knows them.

Headline result: the attack fools the surrogate on 100% of test digits, fools the six targets on 62.3% to 86.8%, and hits the targeted canary on only 20.6% to 48.2%; the three defenses cut transfer by 8.6 to 30.1 points, and an adaptive attacker wins back 14.7 to 19.4 of them. Source: labs/concept-transfer.

By the end you will be able to:

  1. Craft white-box adversarial examples on a surrogate and measure, with confidence intervals, how often they move each of several different targets.
  2. Explain why some targets inherit an attack more than others (gradient alignment, budget, untargeted against targeted) and what an attacker can do to raise transfer.
  3. Grade a transfer claim from evidence on the named target, and re-test a transfer-blunting defense with an adaptive attacker before trusting it.

Safety

Warning

Local sandbox, educational use only. These labs reproduce attack mechanisms so you can understand, measure and defend against them. Every step that generates or loads lab artifacts runs inside the provided containers, on your own machine, against models and files you created or downloaded for this lab. Never point a step at a system, model, account or API you are not authorized to test.

Payloads are harmless by design. Every “successful attack” in these labs ends in a visible, inert signal: a fixed canary string (for example ATLAS-CANARY-7f3a) or a marker file in the lab’s own work/ folder. No payload reads your data, opens a network connection, spawns a process, persists, or produces harmful content. (Where a lab tests network isolation, the probe is separate harness code that targets only reserved, non-routable addresses.) Do not modify payloads.

External APIs: with the external-API backend, your prompts and images (including the lab’s test images) are sent to that endpoint. Prefer a server you run yourself. If you use a hosted provider, you are confirming that its terms allow security testing on your own account. Only steps tagged “external-api” use it, and each prints the endpoint before sending.

Every model in this lab is trained or loaded inside the workbench container from scikit-learn’s bundled digits and the repository’s vision-zoo checkpoints. The targeted attack always aims at the fixed class 3, reported as the canary CANARY-XFER-3, and has no option to change it. No step downloads anything, calls a model server or sends an image anywhere.

What to notice: every target is fooled far more often than by noise, yet less often than the surrogate, and the targeted canary loses most of its strength on the way (80.5% on the surrogate, 20.6% to 48.2% on the targets).

Why

This table is the evidence concept-transfer asks for: one source, six named targets, one budget, with intervals. Untargeted transfer is real here even to models of other families, such as the RBF SVM (whose smooth output the attack never differentiates) and the random forest (whose piecewise-constant output has no useful gradient). That is why a surrogate is a practical attack path when an API hides the weights (LAB-09 shows how an attacker builds one by querying). The targeted rate is the one that matters for a goal like “make it say 3”, and it falls to a fifth on the vision transformer and the forest.

What to notice: the same CNN with another seed has an input gradient that points most like the surrogate’s (cosine 0.708) and inherits the most, the vision transformer’s barely lines up (0.150) and inherits the least, and the budget matters as much as the model: at ε 0.1 no target exceeds 30.1% (the forest), and at ε 0.3 every target is above 90%.

Why

PGD follows the sign of the surrogate’s input gradient, so an image built that way also raises a target’s loss to the extent that the target’s gradient points the same way. The “same sign” column counts the pixels where the two gradients agree in sign, and it orders the four targets the lab differentiates the way the transfer matrix does. Demontis et al. (2019) name gradient alignment as one of three factors in transfer, together with how vulnerable the target is (the size of its input gradient) and how smooth the surrogate’s loss is. It is still a description of these four models: the lab computes no gradient for the SVM, the forest has none worth comparing, and the cosine column puts the MLP and logistic regression in the other order (concept-gradients).

RESULT the ensemble raises mean untargeted transfer 72.7% -> 80.0% (largest gain logreg, +12.6 points) and targeted 31.8% -> 35.6%; momentum barely moves it (72.1%) and input diversity lowers it (68.1%) on 8x8 digits (68 s)

What to notice: attacking several surrogates at once raises transfer to five of the six targets (the exception is cnn-b, whose closest relative cnn-a now shares the vote with less similar models), while momentum changes almost nothing and input diversity lowers transfer on every target.

Why

Liu et al. (2017) showed that an image that fools several models at once is more likely to fool another one, and the ensemble here is leave-one-out, so no target ever helps build its own images. Momentum (Dong et al. 2018) and input diversity (Xie et al. 2019) were developed on 299×299 ImageNet photos. There, resizing and padding by a few pixels barely changes the picture; on an 8×8 digit a one-pixel shift moves the image by an eighth of its width, and input diversity works against the attacker. Momentum, which smooths the step direction across iterations, changes the mean by less than a point here. That is the eval-transfer point turned on attack methods themselves: a technique’s published gain was measured in its own setting, and yours needs its own measurement.

Step 6 — A compatible surface is a hypothesis · explainer 6

Read

Before Step 3, every target looked like a good candidate: the same task, the same 8×8 input with pixels in [0, 1], at least 95.7% agreement with the surrogate on clean digits. That is architectural applicability in the sense of concept-adjacency: the attack has the right input channel and the right output to aim at. The matrix is the evidence, and it splits the one hypothesis into two answers. Untargeted transfer holds for all six targets; the targeted canary reaches at most 48.2% of digits on any of them and 20.6% on two. Neither answer could be read off the surface. Other labs in the Atlas give the same lesson across modalities. In LAB-02 Step 6, a plain PGD image that makes SmolVLM print its canary reaches neither qwen2.5vl:3b nor moondream:1.8b (0/5 each). In LAB-21 Step 7, a GCG suffix built on SmolLM2-135M works on neither held-out model (0/2), not even the 360M model from the same family. Those small samples are evidence of weak transfer, and D1 measures how much a 0/5 can still hide.

Why

A catalog entry such as “this technique targets decoder-only models” or “adversarial images transfer between classifiers” is a place to look, and concept-adjacency asks for a reproduction on the named target before it sets severity. attack-transfer-prompts adds the threat-model half of the same point: an attacker who receives a precomputed suffix or image has different capabilities from one who optimized it against the target.

What to notice: graded on the targets, the untargeted claim holds for all six and the targeted canary claim holds for none, the surrogate’s own rate would have overstated the targets by 13.2 to 60.0 points, and five images per target leave 10 of the 12 claims undecided (the first five vision-transformer digits read 1/5 where the full rate is 62.3%).

Why

This is the first defense step from eval-transfer: source-model success and target-model transfer are different results, so a report names the target, the number of trials and an interval for each claim, and reads the target’s rate from the target. The same check serves both sides. A red team uses it before writing “transfers to X”, and a defender runs it against their own deployed model, because no surrogate number answers “is my model affected?”. Limitation: the grade is only as good as the sample; a 0/5 like LAB-02’s still allows up to 43.4% (its upper bound), so a low-transfer finding needs more trials before it becomes “does not transfer”.

Step D2 — Blunt transfer, then attack adaptively · explainer 8

RESULT the three defenses cut transfer by 8.6-30.1 points (best: (b) + quantize to 3 levels, 56.7%); the adaptive attacker wins back 14.7-19.4 points and reaches 75.2%-92.8% (28 s)

What to notice: each defense lowers transfer to cnn-b (quantization the most, from 86.8% to 56.7%), and an attacker who knows the defense and holds the weights wins back 14.7 to 19.4 points of that. At ε 0.3 the quantizer even helps the attacker: transfer to the quantized cnn-b is 100.0%, against 99.1% for the undefended cnn-b in Step 4, because a pixel pushed to 0.3 rounds up to 0.5.

Why

Random noise, quantization and a vote of unlike models change the target’s inputs or decision so that images tuned on someone else’s model miss more often, which is the “model diversity and input transforms” mitigation in eval-transfer. The adaptive column is the re-test eval-adaptive requires: put the noise inside the attack loop with a fresh draw at every step, so the steps average it out (EOT), pass the rounding step straight through on the backward pass (BPDA, Athalye et al. 2018), attack the logits (the raw class scores before softmax) of the vote’s differentiable members, cnn-b and vit. Limitation: these defenses mainly stop transfer, and an attacker with white-box access (a leaked checkpoint, an insider, or a surrogate good enough to be one) is not stopped; the 24.8% of digits that survive the adaptive attack on the quantizer survive because the rounding thresholds sit at 0.25 and 0.75, so a pixel at 0 or 1 (most of a digit) cannot change its rounded value with a change of 0.2. At ε 0.3 it can, and rounding then pushes the change further. Changing the model itself with adversarial training (defense-robust-train, LAB-50 D2) is the defense that also has to hold white-box.
