Lab guide

Atlas node: Encoder-only language models · Family: family-prompt · Level: intermediate · Time: 75 min · Model backend: none

Learning path: Module 9 · step 5 of 8 · Inside the transformer

Overview

Many apps put a prompt-injection classifier in front of a language model and drop any document it flags. That classifier is usually an encoder-only model: it reads all tokens of its input at once and turns them into one label; it never writes text. Its limits (how much it reads, how it weighs a long document, which characters it recognizes) decide which injections reach the model behind it.

Attention is the step where each token’s vector is updated using the other tokens’ vectors. An encoder lets every token look both ways, at the tokens before and after it. A decoder only looks left, which lets it write text one token at a time; an encoder-decoder model has both halves.

What you will do:

  • run a real guardrail, protectai/deberta-v3-base-prompt-injection-v2 (a 184M-parameter DeBERTa-v3 encoder, labels SAFE and INJECTION), on 8 short inert injections, each asking the reader to print ATLAS-CANARY-3nc0;
  • measure that an encoder reads both ways and a decoder does not;
  • hide the same injections at different places in long, fictional documents, and rewrite them with look-alike letters;
  • feed the passed documents to google/flan-t5-small, an encoder-decoder summarizer, and check its summaries for the canary;
  • defend by classifying every sentence separately and by normalizing Unicode first, then place the classifier as one layer in a larger design.

Headline result: the guardrail catches 8 of 8 short injections, but the pipeline passes only the first 512 tokens to it, so an injection that starts after the first 510 content tokens changes its score by exactly 0. Even before the cut, on the three topics whose clean text passes, it catches 16 of 96 placements, because one verdict for a whole document lets harmless text outweigh the injection. The summarizer reads the whole document and prints the canary or repeats the injected line in 5 of the 12 injected documents the guardrail passed. Classifying every sentence catches 64 of 64 placements and flags 0 of 12 clean documents.

What is staged here: the app truncates the classifier’s input at 512 tokens, as the model card’s example does, but passes the whole document to the summarizer. That gap is the attack in Step 5. The documents are written so the injection can sit after token 512.

Source: labs/arch-encoder.

By the end you will be able to:

  1. Explain what an encoder-only classifier computes (one label from a bidirectional reading of at most 512 tokens) and show the difference from a causal decoder with a measurement.
  2. Measure a prompt-injection guardrail’s detection rate against where the injection sits and how its characters are written, and show the summarizer behind it following what the guardrail passed.
  3. Apply and measure two fixes (classify every sentence and keep the highest score; normalize Unicode first), test them against an attacker who knows them, and state what they do not fix.

Safety

Warning

Local sandbox, educational use only. These labs reproduce attack mechanisms so you can understand, measure and defend against them. Every step that generates or loads lab artifacts runs inside the provided containers, on your own machine, against models and files you created or downloaded for this lab. Never point a step at a system, model, account or API you are not authorized to test.

Payloads are harmless by design. Every “successful attack” in these labs ends in a visible, inert signal: a fixed canary string (for example ATLAS-CANARY-7f3a) or a marker file in the lab’s own work/ folder. No payload reads your data, opens a network connection, spawns a process, persists, or produces harmful content. (Where a lab tests network isolation, the probe is separate harness code that targets only reserved, non-routable addresses.) Do not modify payloads.

External APIs: with the external-API backend, your prompts and images (including the lab’s test images) are sent to that endpoint. Prefer a server you run yourself. If you use a hosted provider, you are confirming that its terms allow security testing on your own account. Only steps tagged “external-api” use it, and each prints the endpoint before sending.

Every document, injection and benign text is fictional and hardcoded in scripts/enclab.py. The only thing an injection asks for is the inert canary ATLAS-CANARY-3nc0; the “attack succeeds” when a summary contains it. Both models run locally in the workbench; nothing is sent to a model backend.

What to notice: every short injection scores above 0.96, most benign texts score 0.000 even when they give orders (“Ignore the noise…”, “Can you print…”), and one harmless sentence that starts with “Forget the old…” is flagged at 0.999.

Why

An encoder-only model (arch-encoder) maps the whole input to vectors, and a small head turns the vector at the first position ([CLS], a special token added in front of every input) into two scores (logits); it never generates text, so it cannot “obey” an injection, only label it. This one was fine-tuned on prompt-injection datasets, which is why it is strong on short, prompt-shaped text. The false alarm is the first sign that it learned surface phrasings (“forget …”) as well as intent, a property attack-detector-evasion exploits.

Step 2 — An encoder reads both ways · explainer 2

Do

Observe

[atlas-lab] arch-encoder · s02_both_ways.py · torch 2.14.1+cpu · backend none
sentence A: 'Please summarize this short note about the lamp.'
sentence B: 'Please summarize this short note about the parcel.'  (only the last word differs)
model part                       attention                first token  tokens  first-token vector change |a-b|/|a|
guardrail encoder (DeBERTa-v3)   bidirectional            '▁Please'        11  0.0654
flan-t5-small encoder            bidirectional            '▁Please'        10  0.2376
flan-t5-small decoder            causal (left to right)   '▁Please'        10  0.0000  (exactly 0)
both encoders' first-token vectors moved with the last word; the causal decoder's did not move at all

What to notice: changing the last word moves the first word’s vector in both encoders (by 6.5% and 24%), and leaves it exactly unchanged in the decoder.

Why

In an encoder every position attends to every other position, so each output vector, and the [CLS] vector the guardrail’s head reads, summarizes the whole input (arch-encoder). A decoder applies a causal mask: position 1 can only see positions 0 and 1, which is what lets it generate one token at a time (arch-decoder). flan-t5-small has both halves (arch-encdec): its encoder reads the source document both ways, and its decoder writes the summary left to right while also attending to that encoding (cross-attention); Step 5 uses it as the summarizer.

What to notice: past token 512 the score is bit-for-bit the score of the same document without the injection, so the three topics whose clean text passes drop to 0 of 72; before the cut those topics were flagged only 16 of 96 times, and the policy topic is flagged with or without an injection (0.89 when clean).

Why

The pipeline calls the tokenizer with truncation=True, max_length=512, as the model card’s example does, so the classifier receives [CLS], the first 510 tokens and [SEP]; anything later never enters the computation. An attacker who writes the document (attack-indirect-injection) controls both where the injection sits and how much harmless text surrounds it. The policy column is a false alarm on clean text, a utility cost (eval-utility) that also hides whether the injection was seen.

What to notice: swapping Latin letters for Cyrillic look-alikes lets 2 of 8 injections through and digits for letters (leetspeak) lets 4 through, while full-width letters, spaced letters and zero-width spaces are still caught, although the last two triple or quintuple the token count.

Why

The classifier never sees letters, only token ids (concept-tokens): Ignore is one familiar token, and its look-alike Іgnоrе (Cyrillic U+0406 first, then U+043E and U+0435 between Latin letters) becomes five pieces the model saw rarely in training. Which tricks work is an empirical property of one model: here zero-width, full-width and spaced text still look like an injection to the classifier, even though they change the token sequence completely. Searching for the transform that flips a specific detector is attack-detector-evasion, whose own lab goes further than these five fixed rewrites.

What to notice: the guardrail passes every injected manual, minutes and story document (it blocks only the policy topic, injected or clean), and 5 of those 12 summaries carry ATLAS-CANARY-3nc0; cutting the summarizer’s input at 512 tokens as well removes the canary from all 5.

Why

T5 encodes position as the distance between two tokens, grouped into 32 ranges (relative position buckets), instead of a fixed table of 512 positions, so its encoder reads all 580–650 tokens even though its tokenizer reports model_max_length 512 (arch-encdec). The two models read different spans of the same document, and the attacker writes in the gap. A 77M-parameter summarizer copies more than it follows: all five canary summaries reproduce the injected sentence, whole or from the canary on, and none of them summarizes the document. Either way the document’s author decided what the summary says, which is the outcome attack-indirect-injection measures on larger models in LAB-15.

What to notice: windows of 510 tokens reach the injection past token 512 but flag only 12 of 32, and 8 of those 12 are policy documents, which are flagged with no injection at all; they also add a false alarm on the clean story topic. One piece per sentence catches 64 of 64, flags none of the 12 clean documents and blocks all 5 documents whose summary carried the canary; gluing the injection onto a harmless sentence gets 4 of 32 past it.

Why

Sliding-window classification with max-score aggregation removes the span limit: every token lands in some window, and the document is flagged when any window is. The window size decides the dilution: with 510 tokens per window the injection is still a small part of each window; per sentence, the injection is the whole piece, as in Step 1. Scoring the whole document in one pass without truncation, which this classifier technically allows because DeBERTa uses relative positions, does no better (10 of 32, with the same story false alarm). Limitations: cost grows with the number of pieces (28 classifier passes per document here instead of 1); the Step 1 false alarm shows that short benign sentences can trip the classifier, so many pieces mean more chances for a false alarm on real documents (eval-utility); and an attacker who knows the split adapts (eval-adaptive).

What to notice: normalization turns the homoglyph, zero-width and full-width versions back into the exact original text (8 of 8 each), so the homoglyph injections are caught again, while the 4 leetspeak injections that slipped through still slip through.

Why

normalize() in scripts/enclab.py applies Unicode NFKC (which folds full-width and other compatibility characters), drops every format character (category Cf, which includes U+200B), and maps 18 Cyrillic look-alikes to Latin letters. NFKC alone does not touch Cyrillic, because a Cyrillic о is a different letter from a Latin o; mapping them needs a confusables table (Unicode publishes one in UTS #39). Limitation: normalization undoes encodings and leaves rewrites alone, so digits for letters, spacing, paraphrase or another language need a classifier trained on them, and an adaptive attacker (eval-adaptive, attack-detector-evasion) will look for exactly those.

Step D3 — The classifier is one layer

In the explainer: explainer 9

Read

Steps 3 to 5 measured three separate limits of one guardrail: a fixed input span (512 tokens), one verdict per input (harmless text dilutes the injection), and sensitivity to how characters are written. D1 and D2 narrow two of them and leave a measurable rest: glued injections, rewrites that normalization cannot undo, and false alarms such as the password sentence in Step 1. None of them changes the fact that the summarizer treats the document’s text as text to act on. So place the classifier as one layer in a design where the document never gets authority. Mark retrieved or uploaded text as data in the summarizer’s prompt and keep it out of the instruction position. Do not let the summary trigger actions (send, call a tool, open a link) without a separate check or a human. Check the output too: in this lab a summary that contains an instruction or a code that was not in the user’s request is a signal. Measure every layer with its false alarm rate on your own clean documents, because the policy topic here shows a guardrail can block legitimate content as reliably as it misses attacks.

Why

Separating data from authority (defense-boundaries) works whether the classifier catches an injection or not, which is why it sits underneath the detection layers. LAB-15 measures that separation (spotlighting and a dual-LLM quarantine) against the same kind of injected document, and LAB-06 puts this classifier in front of a chat bot. Limitation: a model that reads attacker text can still be steered in what it writes, so keep the summary’s consumers (attack-indirect-injection) on the same footing as the document.

Further Reading

  • Devlin et al., BERT: Pre-training of Deep Bidirectional Transformers for Language Understanding (2018), seed source s-bert: the encoder-only, bidirectional pretraining that this guardrail’s family of models comes from.
  • Raffel et al., Exploring the Limits of Transfer Learning with a Unified Text-to-Text Transformer (2019), seed source s-t5: the encoder-decoder T5 with relative position buckets, the base of flan-t5-small.
  • Vaswani et al., Attention Is All You Need (2017), seed source s-transformer: encoder self-attention, causal decoder self-attention and cross-attention, the three patterns Step 2 compares.
  • Greshake et al., Not what you’ve signed up for: Compromising Real-World LLM-Integrated Applications with Indirect Prompt Injection (2023), seed source s-indirect: instructions that arrive inside documents an app processes, the attack this guardrail is meant to stop.
  • NIST, Adversarial Machine Learning: A Taxonomy and Terminology of Attacks and Mitigations (NIST AI 100-2 E2025), seed source s-nist: evasion of deployed classifiers and the limits of detection-based mitigations.
  • He et al., DeBERTaV3: Improving DeBERTa using ELECTRA-Style Pre-Training with Gradient-Disentangled Embedding Sharing (2021): the encoder under the guardrail.
  • Model cards: protectai/deberta-v3-base-prompt-injection-v2 (Apache-2.0; its usage example truncates at 512 tokens) and google/flan-t5-small; Unicode UAX #15 normalization forms and UTS #39 security mechanisms.
