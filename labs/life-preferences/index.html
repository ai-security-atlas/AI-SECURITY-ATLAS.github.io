Lab guide

Atlas node: Preference data and reward models · Family: family-training · Level: intermediate · Time: 60 min · Model backend: none

Learning path: Module 10 · step 5 of 6 · Data, evaluation integrity and preference training

Overview

After supervised fine-tuning, many assistants are tuned further with preference data: people compare two candidate replies to the same question and mark the better one. A reward model learns to give each reply a number, its reward, that agrees with those choices, and later training (RLHF) pushes the assistant toward replies with high rewards. Whoever supplies some of the comparisons can therefore teach the reward model a value nobody on the team chose, and the accuracy number most teams watch barely shows it.

In this lab you build 1,200 such comparisons for a fictional ferry help desk and fit a Bradley-Terry reward head on a frozen SmolLM2-135M-Instruct. Then a contract batch of 400 comparisons arrives. One of its three labelers always prefers the reply that ends with a promo line carrying the inert canary ATLAS-CANARY-pr3f. You measure how the reward moves, how much poison it takes, how the reward model scores real model replies, and which defenses catch it.

Headline result: the planted labels are 7.8% of the set and move held-out accuracy only from 96% to 93%, while the promo line goes from winning 1 of 40 probe pairs to winning 24 of 40.

What is staged here: the poisoner (ann-08) follows its rule on every pair where exactly one reply carries the promo line. For about half of its 240 comparisons it also picks the pairs itself: pairs where the promo reply is no better by the rubric, so every one of those labels pushes toward the promo line. This gives a clear signal in a small dataset. Step 5 measures the attack by flipping randomly chosen honest labels instead. The reward model is a linear head on a frozen 135M-parameter model, so every refit takes under a second. Production reward models fine-tune the whole network.

This is the stage that preference poisoning, RLHF and DPO build on. Source: labs/life-preferences.

By the end you will be able to:

  1. Build a pairwise comparison set, fit a Bradley-Terry reward model on it, and read what the model pays for with minimal-edit probe pairs.
  2. Measure how a small share of planted labels moves the reward for one phrase while held-out accuracy barely changes, including a dose-response curve and real model replies.
  3. Audit a preference dataset per labeler (agreement and a phrase slice scan) and gate a reward model with a probe suite before anything trains against it.

Safety

Warning

Local sandbox, educational use only. These labs reproduce attack mechanisms so you can understand, measure and defend against them. Every step that generates or loads lab artifacts runs inside the provided containers, on your own machine, against models and files you created or downloaded for this lab. Never point a step at a system, model, account or API you are not authorized to test.

Payloads are harmless by design. Every “successful attack” in these labs ends in a visible, inert signal: a fixed canary string (for example ATLAS-CANARY-7f3a) or a marker file in the lab’s own work/ folder. No payload reads your data, opens a network connection, spawns a process, persists, or produces harmful content. (Where a lab tests network isolation, the probe is separate harness code that targets only reserved, non-routable addresses.) Do not modify payloads.

External APIs: with the external-API backend, your prompts and images (including the lab’s test images) are sent to that endpoint. Prefer a server you run yourself. If you use a hosted provider, you are confirming that its terms allow security testing on your own account. Only steps tagged “external-api” use it, and each prints the endpoint before sending.

The ferry company, its questions, the replies, the annotators and the contract batch are fictional and generated from a fixed seed in scripts/prefs.py. The planted preference rewards a promo line with the inert canary ATLAS-CANARY-pr3f, which is hardcoded with no option to change it. Nothing is fetched except SmolLM2-135M-Instruct from the Hugging Face Hub (Step 2), and every comparison set, feature file and reward head stays in the lab’s work/ folder.

What to notice: each comparison is two replies to one question and a label, the in-house panel agrees with its own rubric 92% of the time (annotators are noisy), and the promo line appears in 89 of 640 replies without being part of the rubric. The promo line is “Members can ask the crew about Harbor Gold perks with code ATLAS-CANARY-pr3f.” The range in brackets, such as [89-94%], is a 95% confidence interval: the spread of rates that fit the count you measured. Every bracket in this lab reads the same way.

Why

Comparisons are the raw material of this stage (life-preferences): Ouyang et al. had labelers rank model outputs, and both RLHF and DPO learn from such pairs. Labelers disagree with each other and with their own guidelines, so the label is noisy by design; here each annotator picks the rubric’s preferred reply with a probability that grows with how much better it is. The QA set (60 pairs every annotator labels) and the held-out pairs on five unseen topics are kept out of training and used in Step 2 and D1.

Step 2 — Fit a Bradley-Terry reward head on the in-house panel (≈1.5–2.5 min, + download) · explainer 2

Do

docker compose run --rm workbench python life-preferences/scripts/s02_reward.py

Observe

[atlas-lab] life-preferences · s02_reward.py · torch 2.14.1+cpu · backend none
model HuggingFaceTB/SmolLM2-135M-Instruct @ 12fd25f, frozen; 4 CPU threads
features: 1000 replies x 1152 numbers (mean of the reply's final hidden states + the last token's) in 94s
reward head: 1152 weights, Bradley-Terry loss + L2 0.02, fitted on 800 in-house comparisons in 0.2s
training pairs ordered like the labels: 93.8% (loss 0.168)
held-out pairs on unseen topics ordered like the rubric: 193/200 96% [93-98%]
example c0001: r(chosen) +7.38, r(rejected) -1.67, P(chosen wins) = sigmoid(+9.05) = 1.00
[ok] wrote work/features.safetensors and work/rm-panel.safetensors   (94s)

What to notice: the head is 1,152 weights on top of a frozen model, it fits in a fraction of a second, and it ranks 193 of 200 held-out pairs on topics it never saw the way the rubric would. L2 0.02 is a small penalty on large weights that keeps the head from fitting the label noise. sigmoid turns a reward difference into a win probability between 0 and 1: a difference of 0 gives 0.5, and a large positive difference such as +9.05 gives almost 1.

Why

A reward model maps a (question, reply) pair to one number r. The Bradley-Terry model says the chosen reply wins with probability sigmoid(r(chosen) − r(rejected)), and fitting minimizes the negative log of that probability over all labels (the loss), the loss Ouyang et al. use for their reward model and the one DPO rewrites (life-rlhf, life-dpo). Production reward models fine-tune the whole network with a score head; this lab freezes SmolLM2-135M and trains only a linear head on its hidden states, the lists of numbers the model computes for each token (embeddings, concept-embeddings), which keeps every fit under a second on a CPU so later steps can refit many times; the rewards have no unit, and only differences between two replies to the same question mean anything.

[ok] wrote work/probe-panel.json

What to notice: the reward model rewards all three things the rubric asked for, with the largest margin for helpfulness (+5.64), and adding the promo line to an otherwise identical reply lowers the reward in 39 of 40 pairs.

Why

Held-out accuracy says how often a reward model agrees with labels; a probe pair says what it pays for, because only one part of the reply changes. The comparisons define “better” for everything trained on this reward (life-preferences), including preferences nobody wrote down: the rubric is silent on promo lines, yet the head learned a negative margin for one. That negative margin is most likely a length effect. The rubric penalizes padded replies, and an appended line makes a reply longer, so the head lowers the reward for any added sentence: the unrelated coupon line loses too (9 of 40, margin −0.51). The in-house panel’s 62% promo-side choices in Step 4 do not contradict this. That rate reflects which replies happened to carry the promo line in those pairs (for example, more helpful ones), since an honest labeler ignores the line itself. The lab has no same-length neutral line probe to separate the two effects. The margins are on the same scale, so helpfulness counts about twice as much as politeness here, close to the rubric’s 2 to 1. The order is not exact: the rubric weights politeness above concision (1 to 0.8), while the head pays slightly more for concision (+2.73 against +2.57). It learned the weights from noisy choices, so expect them to match the rubric only roughly.

What to notice: 94 planted labels (7.8%) lower held-out accuracy by only 3 points, and the two intervals overlap, while the promo probe goes from 1/40 to 24/40 and its mean margin turns positive.

Why

ann-08 follows the rubric like everyone else, except that it always picks the reply with the promo line, and it contributes pairs where that reply has nothing else going for it (same helpfulness, no better on the rubric). That is the attack-preference-poison scenario at its smallest: the attacker controls only its own comparisons (access profile A20). Held-out accuracy averages over every kind of pair, and very few of them hinge on the promo line, so the number teams usually watch hardly moves.

A policy trained against rm-all, by best-of-n sampling or by RLHF, gains reward by ending its replies with ATLAS-CANARY-pr3f. The coupon result is an honest negative for the clean model: on this kind of text its reward for an added line is unreliable, and a policy optimized against it could learn to append lines too.

What to notice: ann-08 is the lowest on agreement (80%) but within 15 points of the median, so agreement alone flags nobody; the slice scan flags ann-08 and none of the other 7 annotators. Its top phrase, “the crew about harbor”, is a 4-word window of the promo line. z is a z-score: how many standard errors the labeler’s rate sits from everyone else’s, so +8.9 is far beyond chance and the scan’s cutoff of 4. The reward model refit without ann-08’s 240 comparisons rejects the promo line again (0/40).

Why

Inter-annotator agreement is the standard quality check for labelers, and it averages over all pairs, so a labeler that is honest on most of them looks normal. The slice scan asks a narrower question for every phrase that sits on one side of a pair: does this labeler pick that side much more often than everyone else does? That is a per-contributor provenance check on feedback (defense-provenance). Limitation: it needs other labelers to see the same phrase, it misses a preference spread across many paraphrases or across several colluding labelers, and it says nothing about intent (a labeler who really likes promo codes would be flagged too).

A promo line rescues a deflection in 0 of 40 pairs for each of the three reward models.

Why

A probe suite is a regression test for a reward model, run before anything is optimized against it (defense-regression): the rubric’s wishes must be rewarded, and lines the rubric never asked for must not raise the reward. It needs no access to the labels, so it also works on a reward model you did not train. Limitation: it only covers the probes you wrote and the kind of text they use; Step 6 showed that both reward models reward an unrelated coupon line on real SmolLM2 replies, a problem this template-based suite does not catch, so a real gate also probes the policy’s own outputs.
