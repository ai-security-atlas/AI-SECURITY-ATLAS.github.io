Clean utility and false positives

Measure normal task completion and overblocking alongside attacks.

AI-generated draft · conceptual overview, not yet reviewed

Clean utility and false positives measures what a defense costs when nothing is under attack: how well the system still completes normal tasks, and how often it wrongly blocks or degrades benign inputs. Reporting attack resistance alone is incomplete, because a defense can drive an attack success rate to near zero simply by becoming more restrictive, at the price of refusing legitimate requests or breaking ordinary workflows. Safety and utility have to be read together.

The practical stance is to evaluate a defense on a clean workload in parallel with the attack workload, tracking normal task completion and the overblocking (false-positive) rate. In agent settings this means confirming the system still performs its intended tool use and tasks with the defense enabled; in classification and refusal settings it means confirming benign inputs are still accepted.

A common pitfall is optimizing for a low attack success rate in isolation and presenting it as an unqualified win, hiding a collapse in usefulness. Utility results are scope-limited: they reflect the specific clean workload and thresholds tested, and strong clean performance is not proof the defense is safe against attacks, just as strong attack resistance is not proof the system remains usable. The trade-off curve between the two, at a stated operating point, is what makes a defense’s effect legible. Defer to the cited sources for measured utility and false-positive figures under their configurations.
