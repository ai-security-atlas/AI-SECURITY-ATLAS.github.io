Membership inference

Requires a meaningful candidate and baseline; membership inference is not full-record reconstruction.

Estimate whether a candidate record was included in a model's training set.

AI-generated draft · conceptual overview, not yet reviewed

Membership inference estimates whether a particular candidate record was included in a model’s training set. It routes toward obtaining protected information: even without recovering data, learning that a specific record was used can itself be sensitive.

Conceptually the technique compares how a model behaves on a candidate versus how it behaves on data it never saw, reading signals from logits, probabilities and observable outputs. It relies on memorization, generalization and duplication: models often respond with slightly more confidence to examples they trained on. The attacker-controlled input is the candidate record and any reference or shadow data used for calibration; the victim-controlled state is the classical predictive ML or neural network model under test.

A necessary framing is that the attack needs a meaningful candidate and a proper baseline — a claim of membership is only interpretable against a comparison of what non-members look like. Membership inference is also narrower than full-record reconstruction: it answers an in-or-out question, not what the record contained.

The candidate mitigation is differentially private training, which bounds any single record’s influence and thereby limits the confidence gap, at a possible cost to utility; it reduces rather than removes signal. Evaluation is framed through attack success and severity, an explicit threat model, and privacy verification and baselines. For measured accuracy relative to baseline, defer to the cited source.
